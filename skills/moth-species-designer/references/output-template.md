# Output template

Use this structure. Scale it to the request: a quick "make me a moth" gets sections 1 to 4, the palette block, and the render brief in a few short paragraphs, and skips the cause map; a full field-guide or worldbuilding request gets everything. Every notable visual trait carries a short "because" so the causal chain is visible.

---

## Species profile

### 1. Snapshot
One line: common name (and optional fictional Latin name), region and habitat, size class and wingspan, activity time, one-sentence look.

### 2. Ecology that drives the look
- Activity time and daytime resting substrate
- Main visual predators and other threats
- Defense strategy (primary, and secondary if any)
- Larval host plant and any sequestered chemistry
- Adult feeding (feeds or non-feeding) and expected lifespan
- Broods per year

### 3. Adult appearance (resting view first, then spread or flash view)
Describe by region, with a reason for each element:
- **Head, eyes, antennae, palps**
- **Thorax** (hair, tufts, crest, color)
- **Abdomen** (shape, banding, tip)
- **Legs**
- **Forewing** (base color, pattern elements, lines, spots, patches, texture, fringe)
- **Hindwing** (upper surface, flash color if any)
- **Underside**
- **Wing shape and tails**
- **Texture and sheen** (matte, silky, powdery, glossy, metallic)
- **Resting posture** and what it exposes or hides

### 4. Variation within the species
- **Sexual dimorphism:** what differs and why
- **Morphs:** list each with frequency and the reason it persists
- **Seasonal or brood forms:** how and why they differ
- **Geographic forms:** clines or subspecies
- **Rare aberrations:** which might appear and roughly how often

### 5. Early stages
- **Egg:** shape, color, how laid
- **Caterpillar:** early and late instar look, host plant link, defense, final-instar color change
- **Pupa:** form, cocoon or naked, color and substrate

### 6. This individual
Age, wear, damage, residue (pollen, dust, mites), and whether it is alive in the field or a pinned specimen.

### 7. Palette block (always include)
Five to eight named colors with approximate hex values, each tagged with where it is used (for example "ash-grey #A7A9A4, forewing base"). Name the pigment or structural source if it matters for fading or iridescence. This is what artists, SVG builders, and game asset makers need.

### 8. Cause map (full-length profiles only)
A short list pairing each major trait with the factor behind it, and a tag for how well supported that factor is: established, plausible, unsourced, or speculative. Mention any borrowed-from-butterflies reasoning. Skip this in short profiles, since the "because" clauses already carry the reasons.

### 9. Render brief (if an image, SVG, or illustration is wanted)
A single paragraph. Image models and quick SVGs handle a few strong features far better than a long list of fine ones, so pick **three or four defining features** (overall silhouette, dominant color or pattern, one signature mark, texture) and put everything else in a short second sentence at most. Include: view and pose, background and substrate, lighting, and condition of the individual, plus any things to avoid (for example "no saturated blue unless structural", "no pristine plastic look"). Fine pattern details (exact line counts, spot positions) belong in the profile, not the brief.

## Worked example

**Request:** "Make me a moth that lives in a cold pine forest."

### 1. Snapshot
**Rimepine Dart** (fictional). Cold boreal pine forest, wingspan 38 to 46 mm, nocturnal and flies late autumn into early winter. A woolly grey-brown moth with a pale frosted look that vanishes against lichen-covered pine bark.

### 2. Ecology that drives the look
- Rests by day on the shaded side of pine trunks, among grey-green lichen. Main visual predator: small woodland birds foraging on bark and some tits that glean trunks in winter.
- Defense: lichen and bark mimic (primary). No hidden flash; plain pale hindwings.
- Larval host: pine needles. Larvae are green and striped to match needles.
- Adult feeds on sap flows; lives about 3 weeks.
- One brood per year; adults fly in the cold. Cold flight selects for heavy fur and a compact body.

### 3. Adult appearance
- **Head and eyes:** small head hidden under a fur-like collar of grey scales; eyes dark brown with orange eyeshine when lit. Because it is nocturnal and needs large light-gathering eyes, but the fur hides much of the eye by day.
- **Antennae:** males bipectinate (feathery), females thread-like. Pheromone-search flight in a sparse, cold forest.
- **Thorax:** thick, woolly, pale grey with brown flecks. Because flight in near-freezing air needs insulation. The hair also softens the outline against bark.
- **Abdomen:** thick, grey-brown, hairy, with a ragged dark tuft at the tip.
- **Forewing:** ash-grey base with a bluish bloom; a rust-brown costal patch near the apex; three jagged black dashes along the antemedial line; a pale kidney-shaped spot edged in black; a faint olive-green smear near the base that reads as moss; a scalloped terminal line. Fringe is checkered grey and white. Because lichen-bearing bark is grey-green with black cracks. Wing edge contrast breaks up the outline (disruptive coloration).
- **Hindwing:** plain pale grey with a smoky border; no flash color. Because a hidden flash would not repay the cost in a cold, low-predator-activity season.
- **Wing shape:** moderately short, rounded wings, with a robust body. Because cold-climate moths are compact and heavy.
- **Texture:** powdery and matte, slightly fluffy at the bases.
- **Resting posture:** wings held roof-like, slightly splayed, body tilted toward the bark so the postmedial line continues across the hindwing and forewing.

### 4. Variation
- **Sexual dimorphism:** males slimmer with feathery antennae and a slight pinkish tint on the thorax; females heavier-bodied with a rounder abdomen and thin antennae. Both have the same forewing pattern.
- **Morphs:** pale (65%), intermediate with rust wash (30%), and dark smoky (5%). The dark morph persists on sooty-bark stands near a historic mill site; elsewhere it stays rare.
- **Seasonal forms:** none (one brood).
- **Geographic forms:** higher-altitude populations are darker at the base and slightly smaller.
- **Aberrations:** roughly 1 in 2,000 are albino-like ghost white; 1 in 5,000 are nearly black.

### 5. Early stages
- **Egg:** small, dome-shaped, laid in rows between needle sheaths; pale green turning grey before hatching.
- **Caterpillar:** first instar pale green with a dark head; later instars pale green with white longitudinal stripes that mimic needles; last instar turns brownish before pupation.
- **Pupa:** shiny dark brown, buried in moss or litter beneath the tree.

### 6. This individual
A male, about five days into its flight, lightly worn: fringe slightly frayed at the termen, a few bald scale patches on the thorax, a small yellow pollen-like dusting of pine pollen on the collar (late season, small), and one tiny orange mite near the left wing base.

### 7. Palette block
- Ash-grey #A9AAA4: forewing base (scattering and melanin mix)
- Bluish bloom #9FA7AD: forewing sheen
- Rust-brown #8C5A3C: costal patch (ommochrome)
- Jet black #1E1E1F: dashes and spot edging (melanin)
- Pale lichen-green #A7B09A: moss smear near base
- Frost cream #E8E5DA: fringe checks and kidney spot

### 8. Cause map
- Woolly thorax and compact body: cold flight (established).
- Grey with olive smear and black dashes: lichen/bark mimic driven by bird predation (established).
- Dark morph at 5% near the mill: industrial melanism logic (established, borrowed from the peppered moth).
- Darker base at altitude: thermal melanism (plausible).
- Mite near wing base: ear/wing-base mites occur on moths (established; individual detail only).

### 9. Render brief
Close macro photo of a woolly ash-grey moth with a rust costal patch and jagged black dashes, wings held roof-like on pale lichen-covered pine bark, soft overcast light, matte powdery scales, lightly worn fringe; avoid a glossy or plastic look.

## Variation prompts for multiple outputs

When the user asks for several species, vary the **ecology axes first** (activity time, substrate, strategy, region) rather than just recoloring. A set of five species should differ in at least three axes each, and at least two should be plain generalists, because that is what nature looks like.
