## 1. Body plans by lineage

| Lineage (common name) | Body and wings | Typical look |
|---|---|---|
| Hawkmoths (Sphingidae) | Heavy spindle body, narrow swept-back forewings, small hindwings, long proboscis, spindle antennae with hooked tip | Grey, brown, olive, pink; bands on abdomen; fast, hovering; larvae with a horn |
| Silk moths (Saturniidae) | Very large, broad wings, furry body, feathery male antennae, vestigial mouthparts | Eyespots, window spots, tails in some, green/buff/rust/orange, short lives |
| Tiger moths (Erebidae: Arctiinae) | Stout body, moderately broad wings | Bold black/white/orange/red/yellow; hairy larvae; warning colors |
| Geometrids (inchworm moths) | Slim body, broad thin wings held flat | Fine wavy lines, pale to mid tones, delicate; emeralds are green |
| Noctuids (owlet moths) | Stout body, narrow forewings, wider hindwings, roof-like rest | Brown/grey with kidney and round spots; the plain generalist |
| Underwings (Catocala, Erebidae) | Stout body, cryptic forewings | Bark pattern above, bright banded hindwings |
| Prominents and kittens (Notodontidae) | Stout, hairy, tufted forewing edge | Brown/grey/cream, projecting scale tooth on trailing edge, often twig-like |
| Tussock and lymantriids | Stout hairy body, feathery male antennae | White, cream, grey, brown; females may be flightless; hairy caterpillars |
| Lasiocampids (eggars, lappets) | Large stout furry body, broad wings | Brown, tan, leaf-like; thick hair |
| Burnet and forester moths (Zygaenidae) | Day-flying, slim body, club-tipped antennae | Metallic dark blue-green with red spots; toxic |
| Clearwings (Sesiidae) | Wasp-like body, narrow transparent wings | Black and yellow banded, glassy wings |
| Hook-tips (Drepanidae) | Slim, hooked forewing tip | Dead-leaf pattern, painted midrib |
| Plume moths (Pterophoridae) | Tiny, long legs, wings split into feathery lobes | Pale brown/buff, T-shape at rest |
| Ghost moths (Hepialidae) | Primitive, short antennae, wings similar front and back | Silvery, buff, or brown; hover display in males |
| Pyralids and crambids | Slim, narrow wings folded close, often held like a long triangle | Straw-yellow, brown, sometimes metallic; grass-moth shape |
| Tortricids (leafrollers) | Small, bell-shaped at rest | Brown with banded or bird-dropping patterns |
| Micromoths (many families) | Tiny, often under 15 mm | Patterned gold, silver, brown; some iridescent |
| Uraniid moths | Day-flying, broad wings, tails in some | Iridescent greens, blues, copper on black |

Use these as pointers, not as a requirement to be a real family. For a fully fictional species, pick the body plan that fits the ecology and say so.

## 2. Size classes (wingspan)

- **Micro:** 4 to 20 mm. Most species by count.
- **Small:** 20 to 30 mm.
- **Medium:** 30 to 50 mm. The most familiar "typical moth" size.
- **Large:** 50 to 100 mm. Hawkmoths, many noctuids, lasiocampids.
- **Giant:** 100 to 300 mm. Atlas moth, white witch, big silk moths. Rare, tropical, and usually non-feeding or heavy feeders.

Size rules: females are usually larger than males in non-feeding species; giant species mostly live in warm, wet, resource-rich regions; cold regions favor small and compact.

## 3. Antennae

- **Males of pheromone-detecting lineages:** pectinate (comb-like) or bipectinate (feathery), more branches with larger plume. Large antennae signal a species where females release scent and males search by air.
- **Females:** thread-like (filiform) or weakly toothed.
- **Hawkmoths:** thickened spindle shape with a small hook at the tip.
- **Burnets and some day-fliers:** clubbed or thickened toward the tip.
- **Microlepidoptera:** thread-like, often very long, banded or tipped in contrasting color.

## 4. Pigment-to-color map

What a moth can actually make:

| Pigment or source | Color | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Melanins | Black, dark brown, tan, grey | Stable; made by the moth; most common; controls dark patterns and melanic forms |
| Ommochromes | Yellow, orange, red, brown | Made by the moth; basis of many orange and red patches; can fade |
| Carotenoids | Yellow, orange | From diet; more common in larvae; adults inherit from larval diet |
| Pterins | White, pale yellow | Rare in moths compared with some butterflies |
| Flavonoids | Cream, pale yellow | Sequestered from plants; pale scales; mostly butterflies, uncertain in moths |
| Bilins | Blue-green | Hemolymph and cuticle pigments of many caterpillars and some adults |
| Labile green pigments | Green wings | Typical of emerald moths and some silk moths; fade quickly with age, light, and death |
| Pigment-free scales | White or pale | Scattering structures give white (cream and white moths) |
| Structural nanostructure | Blue, violet, metallic green, gold, silver, copper, iridescence | No pigment; shifts with angle |

Rules:
- **True saturated blue and violet are structural** in nearly all cases.
- **Pink and rose** are rare and often fade (ommochrome or related); use them sparingly or as hindwing flashes.
- **Green** is either a labile pigment (fades) or structural; do not use stable bright green in a worn moth.
- **Red** must come from ommochrome or similar; reds hide on hindwings of flash species or appear as aposematic patches.
- **White** is easy (scattering) and common.

## 5. Structural color options

- **Multilayer reflector** (as in day-flying uraniid moths): shimmering greens, blues, coppers; angle dependent; day-fliers; sits over dark pigment.
- **Metallic spots** (as in gold-spotted plusiine moths): small gold or silver patches on the forewing, usually as part of a cryptic pattern, shiny at certain angles.
- **Glassy windows:** scale-free or sparse-scale membrane areas; clear or faintly tinted; seen in clearwings and some hawkmoths and silk moths; windows in the hindwing or forewing of silk moths are bordered by dark color.
- **Silky sheen:** fine ridges give a satin look on pale or cream wings (common in white moths).
- **Velvet black:** densely packed dark scales with low reflectance for deep black patches in aposematic or contrast patterns.

## 6. Base rates: what real moths mostly look like

Use these to avoid over-flashy designs. Approximate, qualitative.

- Nocturnal: the large majority. Diurnal species exist in several families and make up a small minority.
- Cryptic brown, grey, buff, cream, or white: the large majority of nocturnal species.
- Bright warning colors: a small minority, mostly tiger moths, burnets, and some others, nearly all toxic.
- Iridescent or metallic: a small minority, mostly day-fliers or hidden or small patches.
- Hindwing flash colors: a modest minority, concentrated in a few groups (underwings, tiger moths, some hawkmoths and silk moths).
- Eyespots: a modest minority, concentrated in silk moths, some hawkmoths, and some noctuids.
- Sexual dimorphism: common in size and antennae, less common in color, rare in extreme wing reduction (but notable in winter moths and bagworms).
- Polymorphism (discrete morphs): present in a minority of species, usually 2 to 3 morphs.
- Worn individuals: most wild adults are at least slightly worn; pristine individuals are the exception.

## 7. Anatomical vocabulary for describing wings

- **Costa:** leading edge of forewing. **Apex:** forewing tip. **Termen:** outer edge. **Tornus:** outer lower corner. **Dorsum:** trailing edge.
- **Basal area:** near the body. **Median area:** middle. **Subterminal and terminal areas:** near outer edge.
- **Antemedial and postmedial lines:** cross-lines bounding the median area.
- **Discal spot:** small mark at the end of the discal cell; in noctuids, a round (orbicular) spot and a kidney-shaped (reniform) spot.
- **Fringe:** hair-like scales along the wing margin; may be checkered or concolorous.
- **Frenulum:** wing-coupling bristle (not visible usually).
- **Thorax:** the middle body section carrying legs and wings; **tegulae** are shoulder covers; **patagia** are collar-like plates; may carry tufts or a crest.
- **Abdomen:** often banded or tufted; claspers or ovipositor visible at the tip when stressed.
- **Palps:** small projections in front of the face; sometimes long and upturned (snout moths).

## 8. Biome notes

Quick palettes and body tendencies by habitat. These are tendencies with many exceptions; state any exception you choose. Tags follow `factor-effects.md`, and the climate-driven tendencies are mostly [P, U].

**Hot desert and semi-arid scrub**
- Palette: sand, buff, pale grey, cream, occasional rusty or slate morphs on dark rock.
- Body: sleek, short hair, often smaller; some large hawkmoths are pale and fast.
- Timing: activity pulses after rain; pupae may wait underground for months.
- Hidden color: pale pink, orange, or yellow hindwing flash is plausible.
- Avoid: dense fur, saturated greens, big bold warning patterns without a toxic host.

**Tropical rainforest**
- Palette: the widest range: leaf-green, dead-leaf browns, lichen greens, iridescent blues and greens (day-fliers), warning reds and oranges, glassy windows.
- Body: most diversity of giants (silk moths, white witch-type), long tails, eyespots, and mimicry rings.
- Pressure: strong predation from birds, lizards, spiders, and bats; high mimicry density, so mimic patterns should match local models.
- Wear: fast, from humidity, rain, and fungus; greens and pinks fade.

**Temperate deciduous woodland**
- Palette: bark greys and browns, lichen green-grey, leaf-litter tans, oak-leaf ochre, with a few bright underwings and tiger moths.
- Body: moderate size and fur; seasonal broods and spring/summer forms are plausible.
- Morphs: melanic and pale forms tied to bark darkness and pollution history.

**Boreal and conifer forest**
- Palette: ash-grey, brown, smoky black, pale lichen-green; pine-bark and needle-matching larvae.
- Body: compact, woolly, short rounded wings; winter-flying species with flightless females are plausible.
- One brood a year is the norm.

**Alpine and arctic**
- Palette: dark bases (heat absorption), brown, grey, black, sometimes pale frosted tones above treeline.
- Body: very furry, small, slow-developing (multi-year caterpillars exist in the high Arctic), reduced flight in some.
- Behavior: basking on dark rocks, day-flying in short summers.

**Grassland and meadow**
- Palette: straw, tan, olive, pale yellow, cream; grass-moth narrow shapes folded close to a stem.
- Day-fliers: burnets and some tiger moths show warning colors in sunny meadows.

**Wetland, riverbank, and mangrove**
- Palette: reed browns, pale straw, grey-green; some white and cream species.
- Body: broad, often pale undersides; larvae may be aquatic in a few lineages (rare; avoid unless intended).

**Islands and isolated habitats**
- Tendencies: odd morphs fixed by drift, size change, loss of flight in females or both sexes, simplified pattern. Choose one distinctive difference rather than many.

**Urban and agricultural**
- Palette: pale grey and buff on walls and lamp posts, dark forms near soot or dark stone, polyphagous pests with green/brown/striped larval forms.
- Behavior: light-attracted individuals are often worn.

**Non-Earth or fantasy habitats**
- Keep the causal chain: name the light source and spectrum, the predator senses (sight, sound, smell), and the surface the creature rests on, then derive color, texture, and shape from those.
