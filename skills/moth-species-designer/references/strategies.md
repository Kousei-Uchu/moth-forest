# Strategy recipes

Each recipe is a bundle of traits that go together because they solve the same survival problem. Pick one primary strategy per species (the visible resting surface follows it), optionally add one secondary strategy on hidden surfaces (usually the hindwing or underside).

## 1. Bark mimic
**Problem:** rests by day on tree trunks in view of birds.
**Look:** grey, brown, or ash forewings with fine mottling; black dashes and zigzag lines; patches of pale blue-grey, olive, or rust that mimic lichen and moss; broken-up outline from contrasting patches at the wing edge (disruptive coloration); wavy cross-lines that continue onto the hindwing when at rest.
**Posture:** flat against bark, wings roof-like or spread; orientation chosen to line up with bark furrows.
**Variation:** high between individuals, with 2 to 3 morphs (pale, dark, intermediate) tied to local bark.
**Hidden surface:** hindwings often plain grey or, in flash species, bright.
**Texture:** matte, powdery.

## 2. Lichen mimic
**Look:** pale green-grey base with black and white speckle, olive-green and yellow-grey patches, ragged crescent shapes, a slightly raised scale tuft on the forewing that looks like a lichen lobe.
**Habitat link:** old trees, rock faces in humid regions.

## 3. Dead-leaf mimic
**Look:** tan, ochre, russet, or brown wings; a painted midrib line from base to apex; oblique side veins; irregular dark spots like fungal blotches; a curled or notched margin; sometimes a hooked or pointed apex like a leaf tip; sometimes pale translucent "window" spots that read as holes.
**Posture:** wings held roof-like or folded to a leaf-like profile, resting among litter or hanging in foliage.
**Shape:** often with a hook-tip or angular outline.

## 4. Living-leaf mimic
**Look:** green wings (usually a labile pigment, fading with age and in preserved specimens), pale vein lines, white or yellow fringe, sometimes small brown "damage" spots.
**Note:** green wings fade to yellow, tan, or white within days; a worn green moth should look off-green.

## 5. Twig or broken-branch mimic
**Look:** cylindrical resting silhouette, with wings wrapped around the body; pale tip like a broken branch end (the "buff" end of birch-twig mimics); wood-grain streaks; sometimes tufted scales on the thorax.
**Posture:** held at an angle to a twig, abdomen slightly raised, immobile.

## 6. Bird-dropping mimic
**Look:** white, cream, and black splotches; a glossy or wet sheen from smooth scales; irregular outline; small size (many micromoths and some noctuids).
**Posture:** flat on a leaf upper surface, wings spread or folded flat.
**Rule:** bird-dropping look suits small, flat moths; it does not fit a large furry species.

## 7. Ground, sand, or rock matcher
**Look:** buff, sand, grey, or whitish; sleek short hair; uniform with fine speckle; paler in arid zones, darker on volcanic or dark rock.
**Variation:** populations on dark and pale substrates can differ distinctly.

## 8. Flash-and-hide (startle)
**Look:** forewings cryptic (bark, leaf, or lichen); hindwings brightly colored (red, orange, yellow, pink, or blue) with a bold black band; the bright color is hidden at rest and flashed on takeoff or when disturbed.
**Logic:** a predator that has learned to look for the cryptic shape is startled by the sudden color, then loses it again when the moth lands and hides the color. This is the real pattern in underwing moths.
**Rule:** the contrast must be extreme between the resting and flash view.

## 9. Eyespot defense
**Look:** one or two concentric ring spots on each hindwing (sometimes forewing), with a pale center like a glint, dark ring, and colored outer ring; often placed where a predator's strike would hit a non-vital area; may be paired with a flash display (forewings pulled forward to reveal hindwings).
**Variants:** large spots deter by startling; small spots near the margin deflect attacks to expendable wing edges.

## 10. Aposematic (warning)
**Look:** black with red, orange, yellow, or white; bold and uniform among individuals; large smooth color blocks, spots, or bands; often a thick body with a contrasting abdomen band; legs and antennae often dark.
**Requires:** toxin source (named host plant or chemistry), diurnal or crepuscular activity or a defense that works at rest, slow flight.
**Larvae:** often bright, hairy, and gregarious, with the same colors.

## 11. Wasp or bee mimic
**Look:** wings partly or mostly transparent (scales lost or reduced), narrow dark border; abdomen banded yellow and black; thin waist illusion from color blocks; wasp-like antennae and legs; sometimes yellow face and pale legs; buzzing flight behavior.
**Rule:** mimic is smaller or equal in size to the model, and the model must live in the same place and season.

## 12. Müllerian ring member
**Look:** shares a pattern with other toxic species in the region: for example a black body with orange-and-black hindwing and white-spotted forewing; patterns converge so closely that species differ mainly in small details (antennae, spot shape).
**Rule:** in a given region only a few ring patterns exist; do not invent a new bold pattern if a local ring should be matched.

## 13. Bat-defense specialist
**Look:** long hindwing tails (sometimes twisted) with spatulate tips and eyespot-like patches to deflect bat strikes; thick fur-like thorax and abdomen that absorbs ultrasound; broad, strongly colored wings may still be cryptic by day.
**Note:** these defenses are acoustic. They determine silhouette and fur, not the color pattern.

## 14. Cold-climate furry specialist
**Look:** dense long hair covering thorax and abdomen; dark basal wing areas; shorter, rounder wings; small body or compact, heavy; may fly in cold seasons (winter moths); females may be flightless.

## 15. Plain generalist (most common real look)
**Look:** brown, grey, buff, or cream forewings with simple pattern: a basal patch, an antemedial and postmedial line, a kidney-shaped spot and a round spot in the middle of the forewing (as in noctuids), a pale terminal line, a subtle fringe. Hindwings plain or pale.
**Reality check:** the majority of moths look like this. Using it as a default keeps outputs believable.

## 16. Flower-visiting day-flier
**Look:** compact, fast, hovering; broad dark body with bands or tufts; narrow forewings; orange or red hindwings in some; long proboscis; pollen dust on head and proboscis; hairy thorax; wing scales may fall off on first flights, leaving clear windows.

## 17. Non-feeding giant (silk moth type)
**Look:** very large wingspan with broad wings; thick, furry body; feathery antennae in males; vestigial mouthparts (no proboscis); big eyespots or transparent window spots; tails in some; short life (about a week), so wear and fraying are expected quickly.
**Colors:** pale green, buff, rust, orange, yellow, pink or brown with contrasting lines.

## 18. Brachypterous or wingless female species
**Look (female):** plump, round body with abdomen dominating; wings tiny stubs or absent; sparse coloration (grey, cream, brown); may be covered in scales or hair shed onto egg masses; stays near the pupal case or the host plant.
**Look (male):** fully winged, slimmer, with large feathery antennae.
**Typical:** winter moths, vapourer, bagworm moths.

## Combining strategies

- Primary strategy sets the **resting surface**; secondary sets the **hidden surface**.
- Good pairs: bark mimic + flash hindwing; leaf mimic + eyespot hindwing; plain generalist + scent tufts on males; furry specialist + bark/lichen pattern.
- Bad pairs: aposematic + bark mimic on the same surface; bird-dropping + large furry; iridescent + lichen mimic on the same surface.
